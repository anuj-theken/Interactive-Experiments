Reader survey

We’d rather give than get

160 readers told us how they gift — who gets the money, the things they won’t buy themselves, and why the person they live with is the hardest to shop for.

The KenSurvey: Aug–Sep 20266 min read

A few weeks ago we asked you a simple question with no simple answer: how do you gift? Not what you buy — though we asked that too — but who you spend on, what you hope a gift does, and what you fall back on when you’re out of time.

160 of you wrote back, many at length. What came through wasn’t a shopping list. It was a set of quiet, slightly contradictory rules most of us seem to live by. We’re generous outward and stingy inward. We believe in books and reach for cash. And the people we know best are the ones we’re most afraid of getting wrong.

01 · The budget

Family first. Yourself, barely.

We asked what share of your yearly gifting budget goes to each kind of person. The answer is a steep hierarchy. of readers send a quarter or more of it to immediate family. Colleagues, extended family and everyone else mostly get the crumbs — 1–5% each.

And you? of readers spend 1–5% of their gifting budget on themselves — roughly what they set aside for a colleague.

02 · The gift gap

We’d spend more on you than we’d let you spend on us

Next, two numbers: the ideal amount to spend on a friend or family member, and the ideal amount for them to spend on you. They should, in theory, be the same. A gift is an exchange.

They weren’t.

Line each reader’s two answers up and the pattern is lopsided. would spend more on others than they want spent on them. keep it even. Just would like to receive more than they give.

The gap holds at every age, but it’s widest among readers in their thirties — the decade of weddings, first homes and new babies, when the median ideal gift (₹5,000) is two and a half times what they’d want for themselves.

03 · The wish list

The things we want, and won’t buy

That inward stinginess shows up again when we asked for a gift you’d love but would never buy yourself. Of the readers who named something, gave the price as the reason — it’s too expensive, too costly, a luxury. Others couldn’t justify it, felt guilty, or decided it was an indulgence.

The objects themselves are modest more often than you’d think: a bicycle, scented candles, a toaster, weekly flowers, a reading chair.

Which is, perhaps, the whole point of a gift. Several readers described their best recent present as precisely the thing they’d been refusing themselves.

04 · The calendar

Birthdays, and then everything else

Nearly everyone gifts on birthdays. After that, the list falls off quickly — weddings, housewarmings and anniversaries for about half of you; Diwali for fewer than four in ten. A typical reader ticked five occasions.

But the occasion, many of you said, is the least interesting part. Some of the strongest opinions in the survey were against it.

05 · The point

Younger readers want to be seen. Older ones want to be surprised.

What should a gift make someone feel? The most common answer overall was cared-for. But split readers at 40 and the second place changes hands. Under 40, seen & understood is a close second. Over 40, it drops to last and delighted or surprised takes the lead.

We also asked you to finish a sentence. Read enough of the answers and they sort into a few camps: a gift should get used, it should be remembered, it should bring joy, it should show you know them. The best ones manage all four.

06 · The hardest person

The people closest to you are the hardest to buy for

We asked who you find hardest to buy for. Not the boss, not the colleague you barely know. Overwhelmingly, it’s the two people at the top of your budget: your partner and your parents, tied at answers each. (“Wife” came up times; “husband” .)

The reasons, though, are opposites. Partners are hard because you know them too well — every safe idea is used up and expectations only rise. Parents are hard because they won’t let you: they claim to need nothing and scold you for spending.

07 · The fallback

We believe in books. We fall back on cash.

Ask readers of The Ken for a gift that never misses and you will, unsurprisingly, hear “books” — named them. But ask what they actually do when there’s no time and no energy, and the answer shifts. Books still lead, narrowly. Cash, vouchers and gift cards jump from an afterthought to a near-tie.

Not everyone is a believer, either.

08 · The good stuff

What the best gifts had in common

Finally, we asked for the best gift you’ve ever given, and the best you’ve received recently. Almost none of the answers were expensive. A lot of them were handmade. Nearly all of them were proof that someone had been paying attention — sometimes for years.

And because we couldn’t resist, we asked you to dream. An extravagant gift you’d love, fat chance or not. Some of you priced it to the rupee.

One reader’s whole philosophy, in eight words

“Buy what they wouldn’t, wrap it like they would.”

About this survey. 160 readers of The Ken responded to an open survey between 16 August and 14 September 2026. It is a self-selected sample, not a representative one — skewed, as you’d expect, towards readers in their thirties and forties.

Budget figures are what readers called ideal, not what they actually spend. Categories for free-text answers (hardest person, safe bets, fallbacks, “a great gift is one that…”) were coded by keyword and are approximate. Quotes are lightly edited for length and clarity, and appear only where readers agreed to be published; names appear only where readers asked to be credited.
