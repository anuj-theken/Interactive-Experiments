Weatherproof

The retirement shield against Trump’s war and market meltdowns

By Anand Kalyanaraman

Your retirement may not survive its first bad year. This number could help

8 Apr 2026  /  9 min read

  Comment

Anand is Finance Editor at The Ken. A Chartered Accountant, he chose to pack the power of numbers with words when he left a career of seven years in accounting, putting together MIS reports, and investment research to enter journalism.

  • The just-retired and soon-to-retire have to brace for bad portfolio returns in the initial years
  • The latest shock to the market from the war in West Asia could have a deleterious cascade effect, felt well into their retirement horizons
  • Careful planning with a correct “safe withdrawal rate” could help offset the damage
  • It’s crucial to have the right corpus amount too or to manage the portfolio with other hacks

Investors, from time to time, have to weather market shocks. It’s the nature of the beast.

Just as markets were settling from the volatility set off by tariffs this past year, and Covid a few years before that, the war in West Asia is sending stocks crashing. Indian indices have already suffered a 10% shave, and with hostilities escalatingCNBCTrump warns Iran’s ‘whole civilization will die tonight’ unless deal struck, things could get worse.

This pain to the piggy bank will be felt more acutely by retirees. A sharp hit to their retirement corpus—on which they depend for regular expenses—will hurt, as they may not have the time to wait for the stock market to recoup.

The just-retired and soon-to-retire also face a nasty, often overlooked shock: the sequence-of-returns risk.

Put simply, the order in which returns arrive matters as much as the returns themselves. Returns from equity vary widely year to year. So, losses early on during the retirement, when withdrawals have already begun, can shrink a portfolio disproportionately. That’s because it leaves less capital to participate in any recovery. The case is no better even if long-term average returns look healthy.

The result is an unpleasant possibility of outliving one’s retirement corpus.

To offset this, experts recommend using the safe withdrawal rate (SWR). “This is essentially how much you can withdraw in the first year from a retirement corpus, and top up with inflation in each subsequent year, without exhausting the corpus prematurely,” said Ravi Saraogi, a registered investment adviser and co-founder of financial-planning firm Samasthiti Advisors.

Take someone retiring at the age of 60 with a corpus of Rs 1 crore and a 30-year horizon. A safe withdrawal rate of about 3.3% would mean withdrawing roughly Rs 3.3 lakh in the first year, and increasing that amount with inflation each year.

If she were to withdraw Rs 4 lakh instead in the first year, the money is likely to run out well before she reaches 90 years.

SWR calculations in reality, though, are far more complex. They factor in inflation, the asset-allocation mix, expected portfolio returns, annual expenses, and the number of years in retirement, among other factors. These assumptions and numbers are then run through thousands of simulations.

In the US, most people stick to a safe withdrawal rate of 4%. But applying the same to India would be incorrect, argued Saraogi and Rajan Raju, an IIM-Ahmedabad alumnus and ex-banker who runs Invespar Pte, a Singapore-based single-family office.

Based on their studiesSSRNBalancing Acts: Safe Withdrawal Rates in the Indian Context, the different market conditions and volatility in India make a broad 3–3.5% SWR range appropriate. 

The difference may look small. Over decades, it is often the difference between a corpus lasting and one that runs out.

Better safe than sorry

It’s not the first time the Indian stock market has been reeling under a shock, nor will it be the last. Major events—from the Harshad Mehta scam in 1992 to Covid in 2020—have sent stocks crashing, triggering sequence-of-returns risk for many retirees, even if they were not aware of what had hit them.

Saraogi analysed these black swan shocks of the past three decades and their influence on the retirement corpus in his recent research. He found that correct, conservative math by the retirees or their advisors would have helped them sail through adverse events with at least a 95% success rate95% success rateIn statistical terms, the SWR simulations have a confidence interval of 95%. The SWR is considered successful if the failure rate is less than 5%.

What does this look like in real life?

A retiree in 1992, when the Harshad Mehta scam broke and triggered a 50% market crash over a year, would have held up over a 30-year period had she capped her SWR to about 3.5% or less.

Pushing the number to 4% would have exhausted her corpus by the 25th year.

A similar pattern emerges for a person who’d have retired in January 2008, just as the global financial crisis was setting in. The event sent the market crashing by 60% in a year. A 3% SWR would leave the retiree unscathed, but hiking it up to 3.5–4% could lead to a prematurely empty portfolio.

A Covid crash analysis, on the other hand, shows that while a 3–3.5% SWR would see a retiree make it home over a 30-year period, a 4% SWR could result in a failure.

The model uses actual data through December 2025 and simulates thousands of scenarios based on past data, including the Sensex, fixed-deposit rates, and consumer-price index, to project returns and inflation for the remaining retirement years.

“Those who say that high SWRs of 6–7% are feasible are way off the mark and could end up hurting retirees’ prospects,” said Saraogi.

He also advocated for keeping the minimum retirement period at 30 years, especially with life spans increasing. “The cost of failure with aggressive assumptions of a lower retirement period of say, 20–25 years, can be steep,” he added.

Even relatively modest SWRs of 3–3.5% come with an important, seemingly counterintuitive assumption—on an asset class that can give higher returns but comes with higher risks.   

More equity, more risk

The share of equity in a retiree’s portfolio has to be kept moderate, at 50% or less.

That’s because increasing equity does not increase the SWR. Instead, its inherent volatility plays spoilsport and increases the sequence-of-returns risk impact, potentially jeopardising the chances of a successful outcome for the retiree.

For instance, if someone who retired during the 2008 financial crisis had a rather larger equity allocation in their corpus, of say, 70% instead of 50%, the outcome would have been a failure even with a modest SWR of 3%.

And someone who retired during the 1992 Harshad Mehta scam would not have recovered from the adverse sequence-of-returns hit, even with a moderate SWR of 3.5%, if the equity exposure was 60%.

“It is impossible to predict how the market will move in the short-to-medium term. So, five to seven years before retirement, one should start pruning the risk from a high equity exposure,” said Saraogi.

Rebalancing should continue even after retirement to maintain the original asset mix, say as an even split between equity and debt. Even as the corpus keeps changing with market movements and the asset mix gets rebalanced, the SWR remains anchored as the withdrawal in the first year. Subsequent years’ withdrawals are only adjusted for inflation.

But since wrapping one’s head around complex concepts like simulation can be challenging for most people, Saraogi has distilled the processSamasthiti AdvisorsSafe withdrawal rates of arriving at the suitable SWR to just two variables: equity allocation and retirement period. 

For instance, the SWR for a portfolio with an equity allocation of 50% and a 30-year retirement period, would be 3.46%.

Along with SWR, having the right corpus is integral to a successful retirement planning success. And here, being realistic will go a longer way.

Better realistic than simplistic

The starting point to arrive at the right corpus is the expected annual expenses in the first year of retirement. Say that number is Rs 12 lakh.

Under a “stochastic model”, one that runs thousands of simulations across different combinations of returns and inflation, the required corpus comes to over Rs 3.8 crore.

A “deterministic model”, by contrast, assumes steady returns, at rates that are generally accepted, each year—say 12% for equity, 7% for debt, and 5% inflation—and throws up a far lower estimate of Rs 2.4 crore.

Only when a lot more conservative assumptions, of blended returns of about 7% and inflation of 6%, are used does the deterministic model’s outcome approach the stochastic one.

Financial planners and calculators typically use the deterministic model. But the problem, according to Saraogi, is that it does not reflect reality. Returns and inflation do not move in straight lines. More importantly, they ignore the sequence-of-returns risk altogether, raising the chances of failure.

[The deterministic model] assumes that the portfolio will earn the same equity and debt returns every year, and will have to withstand the same inflation. But the reality will be very different

— Ravi Saraogi, co-founder, Samasthiti Advisors

What makes the stochastic model more reliable is that it tests portfolios against a wide range of possible futures.

But not all investors can invest and build an ideal retirement corpus that can run into crores of rupees. “For such investors, dynamic withdrawal strategies can provide possible solutions to deal with shocks such as an adverse sequence of returns,” Saraogi said.

With dynamic strategiesThe Ken'Dynamic withdrawals' to the retiree’s rescue?, withdrawals can vary based on inflation, market conditions, and the health of the portfolio. A popular strategy is the “forego inflation” approach, where retirees skip increasing withdrawals after a bad year to help their corpus last longer.

Others, like “bucket strategy” and “guardrails”, also factor in risk tolerances and lifestyle needs, with the goal of reducing portfolio failures. But these approaches come at a cost: retirees must let go of the idea of a steady, inflation-linked income and accept that withdrawals might be volatile, varying from year to year.

Retirement planning, then, is less about optimising returns and more about ensuring survival. The real cost of getting it wrong is simple and irreversible: running out of money.
